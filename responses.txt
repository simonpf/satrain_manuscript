* Reviewer #1

This paper developed SatRain, the first AI-ready benchmark dataset for
satellite-based detection and estimation of rain, snow, graupel, and hail.
SatRain includes multi-sensor satellite observations representative of the major
platforms currently used in precipitation remote sensing, paired with
high-quality reference estimates from ground-based radars corrected using rain
gauge measurements. The topic is very interesting and has important implications
in prediction of urban weather. The paper is well organized and written.The
findings of this study are worth of publication in the SD after revision as
following: There are various types of global precipitation, such as convective
rain, frontal rain, orographic rain, and typhoon rain. When the author cited
examples to verify the reliability of the dataset, the current results only
included typhoon-related precipitation cases and their spatial distribution,
which is insufficient. In fact, it is essential to fully assess whether this
precipitation dataset can capture the temporal and spatial variation
characteristics of these different precipitation types, thereby demonstrating
the robustness of the dataset's results. Particular attention should be paid to
whether the dataset can effectively characterize locally intense convective
precipitation, which has a small spatial scale and strong suddenness.


** Respose
We would like to thank the review for providing feedback on the manuscript.

We agree with the reviewer that it is essential for the SatRain dataset to cover
a wide range of different precipitation processes. Because of this we have
designed the dataset to cover more than five years of data across various
regions around the Earth. The results shown in Fig.~8 are meant only one of the more than 800 overpasses from the test data from the Korea domain. The retrieval evaluation shown in Fig.~9 covers 6_00, 8_00, 2_000 overpasses over the Austria, Korea, and CONUS domains respectively coverin a wide range of different precipitation events.

To make this point clear, we have added an additional case study to the
manuscript showing a convective precipitation event over the eastern CONUS. In
addition to this, we have reformulated the description of the results in Fig.~9
to clearly highlight that the evaluation covers a wide range of precipitation
events.

* Reviewer #2 (Remarks to the Authors (required)):

SDATA-25-05168: A Benchmark Dataset for Satellite-Based Estimation and Detection
of Rain This manuscript describes SatRain as the first AI-ready, standardized
dataset that pairs multi-sensor satellite observations with high-quality
ground-based radar precipitation estimates.

The paper provides background on the challenges of global precipitation
measurement and the need for a unified benchmark. It outlines the dataset’s
content: GMI and ATMS observations, multi-channel geostationary imagery (e.g.,
GOES-16 ABI), ancillary data, and gauge-corrected radar reference precipitation
(MRMS over the majority of CONUS).

SatRain covers 4 years of training/validation data and includes independent test
sets over South Korea and an Austrian gauge network, enabling evaluation outside
of CONUS. They also conduct technical validation experiments, training baseline
machine-learning models (random forest, MLP, CNN, etc.) on SatRain and
benchmarking them against traditional methods.

The results demonstrate that models trained on SatRain can outperform
conventional retrievals and maintain performance across regions and sensors,
highlighting the dataset’s value. Overall, SatRain addresses a clear gap in the
precipitation remote sensing community by providing an open, well-documented
dataset to accelerate the development and comparison of ML precipitation
retrievals. I do not see any fundamental weaknesses that require rethinking the
approach, only minor improvements that can be made in a straightforward
revision.

** Response

We would like to thank the reviewer for their thorough feedback on our manuscript.

The writing of the paper gives the impression that SatRain is global
or CONUS. Since the training domain excludes western CONUS, a region known for
challenging precipitation retrieval due to orographic precipitation and Pacific
jet stream influences. As a result, AI models trained solely on SatRain will
inevitably reflect central and eastern CONUS precipitation characteristics,
limiting generalization. The authors should explicitly acknowledge this
limitation in both the abstract and title and clearly position SatRain as
primarily a benchmark dataset over limited area, not a CONUS or globally
representative training resource. I suggest clearly acknowledge that
generalization beyond training regimes can be limited (even with non-CONUS test
sets), and that western CONUS and other complex orographic regions remain a
challenge.

We agree with the reviewer that the generalization of retrievals trained using
the SatRain data cannot be guaranteed and is an important limitation for the use
of the dataset. It is not the case, however, that the SatRain dataset covers
only western CONUS. We suspect that this misunderstanding originates from the
fact that Figures 1 to 5 are cetnered on the western part of the domain. We have
add a note to the manuscript to clarify that these figures are centered on the
overpass but that the dataset covers all of CONUS.

In Limitations, emphasize, snowfall uncertainty (gauges; MRMS
gauge-correction not applied to snow), orographic precipitation challenges (beam
blockage, mixed-phase microphysics), regional regime differences (e.g., warm
rain vs. mixed-phase).

We have added these very important points to the limitations section.


This reads as honest and practical guidance for users. The choice of GMI (high
information content) and ATMS (coarser, fewer precip-sensitive channels) nicely
cover the idea of multiple instruments, but many products rely on a
constellation. Expanding to additional PMW sensors would allow sensor-agnostic
training and stronger cross-sensor robustness testing.

Add to Future Directions a concrete plan for adding more PMW sensors, offering
either a unified sensor-agnostic representation or sensor-specific subsets, and
providing inter-sensor calibration/harmonization guidance. One suggestion is to
mention widely-used multi-satellite precipitation products like NASA’s IMERG in
the background, to further contextualize the state of the art. IMERG (Huffman et
al. 2020) is a flagship global precipitation product that merges satellite
retrievals and noting its existence (and the fact that it relies on underlying
retrieval algorithms, GPROF, PERSIANN) would strengthen the motivation for
SatRain.



The technical design of SatRain is excellent and largely meets
Scientific Data standards for a dataset description. The authors clearly
describe the data sources and processing, all satellite observations are
carefully collocated to each passive microwave overpass time, and are provided
in two spatial formats (native swath vs. a common grid). This dual-format
approach smartly accommodates both pixel-wise methods and those requiring
gridded imagery, ensuring “AI-readiness”.

There is a minor inconsistency about
whether year 2018 is included in training: “The SatRain training data is derived
from four years (2018-2021) of ground based radar measurements over CONUS ….
Training and validation data are extracted from the years 2019 through 2021”.
Please clarify this.

Clearly restate the split policy in one place. Avoid split
info scattered in multiple sections.

The case study and cross-domain metrics convincingly show estimation skill.
Since detection tasks (rain/no rain, heavy rain) are part of the benchmark,
include (even briefly) a quantitative detection result (e.g., HSS or PR AUC) for
at least one model vs. a baseline. The baselines deliberately exclude ancillary
data to isolate sensor contributions. It would help to note (in Usage
Notes/Future Directions) that adding ancillary variables often improves
retrieval skill and that SatRain enables those studies (even if you don’t
present them here).

Use consistent forms (e.g., CONUS vs US, ERA5 vs “ERA-5”; NEXRAD capitalization,
and many more).
